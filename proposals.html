Where would trees matter most?

Tree-planting proposals ranked by a year of air-quality data from our map — with the honest arithmetic on what trees can and cannot do for air and carbon.

Quantum Ethic · part of our air-quality project, started summer 2025 · first published October 2026 · the rankings below update automatically whenever the map's history is rebuilt.

In one paragraph. We ranked the communities on our Air Quality Map by their modeled fine-particle pollution (PM2.5) over the past year — average level, number of unhealthy days, and the worst day — and read each city's own canopy plans. The cities that combine high PM2.5 with thin canopy are where new trees do the most good, and inside those cities the gain is largest in the lowest-canopy neighborhoods next to the busiest roads. We propose that each priority city direct its next planting cycle there, choose species that don't make ozone worse, and measure the result with a cheap sensor before and after. We also say plainly what trees won't do: at city scale they trim PM2.5 by well under one percent. Their real air-quality value is local — the block, the schoolyard, the bus stop — plus shade, cooler streets, and carbon stored over decades.

2. How we rank

Two things decide where a tree helps most: how bad the air is, and how little canopy there already is. We take the data ranking above as the first cut, then read it against each city's own canopy assessment and planting plan. Where a city's canopy is already high (Atlanta, Washington), the marginal tree matters less for air; where it is thin and the air is bad (Phoenix, Los Angeles, Salt Lake City, Denver, Houston in a typical year), it matters most. Within any city, the right place is the same: the census tracts with the least canopy, adjacent to freeways and major arterials, where both pollution and heat concentrate and where residents tend to have the least say over what gets planted.

4. What trees can and cannot do for air

The honest number. In the most complete national study, trees and forests removed about 17.4 million tonnes of air pollution across the United States in 2010, but the average improvement in PM2.5 concentration from urban trees was well under one percent — on the order of a quarter of a percent in cities (Nowak et al., 2014). Trees do not fix a city's PM2.5. Wildfire smoke, traffic, and wood burning do that, and only emission cuts undo it.

Where trees do work. Locally. Leaf area captures particles and a tree line between a road and a sidewalk, schoolyard, or playground measurably lowers exposure right there, where people breathe. Shade cuts surface temperatures by tens of degrees Fahrenheit, which lowers heat deaths and cuts the air-conditioning load, and cooler air forms less ozone. That is why we propose where to plant rather than just how many.

The ozone caveat. Some species emit large amounts of isoprene and other volatile organics that react in sunlight to form ozone — the summer pollutant that dominates in Salt Lake City, Denver, Phoenix and Los Angeles. High emitters include sweetgum, many oaks, poplars and eucalyptus; low emitters include maples, lindens, elms, hackberry, and many fruit trees. In ozone-limited cities, species choice is part of the air-quality proposal, not landscaping detail.

5. The carbon arithmetic

The EPA's greenhouse-gas equivalencies use 0.060 metric tons of CO₂ sequestered per urban tree seedling grown for ten years. A mature tree takes up roughly 48 pounds of CO₂ a year. So a 1,000-tree planting stores about 60 tons of CO₂ in its first decade — about what 13 typical passenger cars emit in a year (4.6 t each, EPA) — and more every year after as the trees grow. That is real but small next to a city's emissions. Planting trees does not reduce emissions; it stores some of the carbon that emissions already put in the air, for as long as the tree stands. We say so because an ethics organization should not sell a tree as a tailpipe.
