How to read it

US AQI is the EPA's 0–500 scale. Under 50 is good; 51–100 is moderate; 101–150 is unhealthy for sensitive groups (asthma, heart or lung disease, children, older adults); above 150 is unhealthy for everyone. The live AQI is the worst of the pollutants at that hour; the historical AQI is computed from each day's mean PM2.5 using the EPA's breakpoints, the same way the official daily PM2.5 AQI is calculated.

PM2.5 is fine particulate matter under 2.5 micrometers — small enough to pass from the lungs into the blood. It is the pollutant most tightly linked to health effects, and the one wildfire smoke, traffic and wood burning produce. The EPA's annual standard is 9 µg/m³ and its 24-hour standard is 35 µg/m³; the WHO guideline is 5.

Methods and limitations

Two sources. The grid, the community values and the history come from the Open-Meteo Air Quality API, which serves hourly output from the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) global model — a model, estimating air quality everywhere, including places with no sensor. The small dots are EPA AirNow monitors — sensors, reporting what was actually measured at that site in the last hour, fetched through our server every 15 minutes. Where a community has a monitor within 60 km, the table shows both side by side; when they disagree, the sensor is the ground truth and the gap is the model's error at that moment.

The model's limits. Its grid is tens of kilometers across, so it cannot see a highway, a wildfire plume's edge, or one neighborhood versus the next. Monitors are sparse outside cities. This map is for seeing patterns at the scale of regions and towns, and for comparing places on the same basis at the same hour or day. The history view uses a file we build from the model — hourly PM2.5 for every grid square and community, averaged into daily values — and rebuild as the record grows; the date range it covers is printed above the chart. For community sensors near you, see the PurpleAir map.

Grids. The live view samples a 2° × 2° grid (about 140 miles across) at each square's center; the history view uses a 4° grid so the file stays small. Both are coarse by design — fine enough to show a smoke event or a stagnant basin, coarse enough to stay honest about what a global model can resolve.

Who built it. Quantum Ethic. We started this project in summer 2025, and the map has been running live since 2026. The pages are plain files with no tracking and no accounts. The written part of this project — Where would trees matter most? — ranks these communities by this history and proposes where each city should plant next, with the honest arithmetic on what trees do for air and carbon.
